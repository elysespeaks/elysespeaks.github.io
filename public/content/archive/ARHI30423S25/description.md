### Topic

This course will explore the history of art in the 20th century from a global perspective, focusing on several locations and movements that were crucial to the development of an avant‑garde and its legacies—including places such as **Paris, Tokyo, Moscow, Berlin, Zurich, London, Rome, Buenos Aires, Rio de Janeiro, New York City, and Mexico City**. The course will examine a wide variety of figures, movements, and practices within the visual arts, situating them within the social, political, and historical contexts in which they arose. The history of these artistic developments (e.g., abstraction, surrealism, muralism, the readymade, conceptual art) will be traced through the rise of mass‑media technologies (such as photography and print media) alongside the aesthetic accomplishments of the avant‑garde. Exploring the forces of feminism, capitalism, and urbanism, we will attempt to understand how artistic innovations fundamentally altered, negotiated, and framed the ways in which we understand and represent the world.

Artists we will look at include **Pablo Picasso, Henri Matisse, Georgia O’Keeffe, Dorothea Lange, Frida Kahlo, Jacob Lawrence, Jackson Pollock, Eva Hesse,** and **Andy Warhol**.  
The curriculum will also include visits to the university museum. This course is a lecture format and largely exam‑based.

### **Learning goals**

After successfully completing this course you should be able to:

* give an informed view about examples of modern artistic practices.
* explain recent ways in which modern art challenges and responds critically to art history.
* understand different approaches to art history and visual subjects.
* recognize ways in which visual culture shapes and affects values, attitudes, and social ideals.
* understand and describe arguments for challenges to traditional artistic formats, media, and standards.
* look critically and take an informed approach to visual subjects.
* think critically and articulate thoughts about visual subjects.

The principal goal is to introduce you to key cultural, social, and visual ideas that form the foundation for artistic practices in the 20th century in order to equip you with (some of!) the knowledge necessary to hold an informed view.   This means learning about the social and cultural climate in which these works were produced as well as about the art‑critical language that has come to define 20th‑century aesthetic production. It also requires exercising a new skill set that is key to understanding visual culture — educated _looking_. In order to learn to look closely and well at objects and images, we will often take class time to describe and examine certain works that you may or may not have encountered before. The point is to challenge you to progress in your ability to use an informed art‑historical vocabulary (and art‑historical eyes) when looking at material culture.
