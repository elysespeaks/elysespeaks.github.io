### Topic

This course offers students an introduction to the theories and practices of contemporary art with a focus on artwork since 1980. We will investigate its varied, multi-faceted terrain, and examine key themes and ideas that have been explored in recent years. These include such topics as the artist as curator, the museum reconsidered, art and politics, the emergence of DIY approaches, and the rise of interest in new media and materials. Special attention will be paid to the way that new media and formats, like digital photography, sound, and installation, have changed the scope and reception of art now.

### Learning goals

After successfully completing this course you should be able to:

- give an informed view about recent examples of contemporary artistic practices.
- explain recent ways in which contemporary art challenges and responds critically to art history.
- understand different approaches to art history and visual subjects.
- recognize ways in which visual culture shapes and affects values, attitudes, and social ideals.
- understand and describe arguments for recent challenges to traditional artistic formats, media, and standards.
- look critically and take an informed approach to visual subjects.
- think critically and articulate thoughts about visual subjects.

The principle goal is to introduce you to key cultural, social, and visual ideas that form the foundation for recent artistic practices in order to equip you with (some of!) the knowledge necessary to hold an informed view of works of art produced in the last thirty years. This means learning about the social and cultural climate in which these works were produced as well as about the art critical language that has come to define 20th- and 21st- aesthetic production. It also requires exercising a new skill set that is key to understanding visual culture -- educated *looking*. In order to learn to look closely and well at objects and images, we will often take class time to describe and examine certain works that you may or may not have encountered before. The point is to challenge you to progress in your ability to use an informed art historical vocabulary (and art historical eyes) when looking at material culture. This objective will be tested in your exams where you will be asked to identify and discuss images in an art historical manner.

A further note on exams and assignments: Because looking is one of the key skills to be developed in this course, the exams will be based entirely around images, and in large part will rely on information only available in lecture. Sometimes I will provide handouts that note some of the factual material covered in class – this is to allow you to better concentrate on the formidable task of learning to describe and analyze works of art. But the *analytic* components that we discuss in class are key to thinking about the work. **Taking good notes is essential to succeeding in this course.** The handouts are not comprehensive and do not cover that element of the material. And the readings supplement, but do not cover, all of course content. So when you are taking notes, concentrate on the kinds of **descriptions and analysis** (description alone is not enough!) of the works of art we focus on in class. Learning how to do this kind of visual analysis for yourselves should be your primary goal in preparing for your exams and other assignments. Other key components to success in this class include doing the readings before class, asking questions, and attending office hours.
